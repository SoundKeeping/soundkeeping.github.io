About

Hello! I’m an interaction researcher and designer interested in how interactive and immersive systems can help people understand complex issues, explore alternatives, and put specialized knowledge into practice. Much of my work has focused on urban soundscapes: how we can help a wider range of people hear, explore, and participate in decisions about how our cities sound.

I’m currently completing a PhD in Information Studies at McGill University. My work in interactive-system design crosses HCI, soundscape, learning, knowledge translation, and professional practice. I’m particularly interested in research that crosses disciplinary boundaries and uses technology to make new forms of understanding and action possible. I also used to be a linguist, which still finds its way into my work in more subtle ways.

Affiliations: McGill School of Information Studies · Multimodal Interaction Lab · Centre for Interdisciplinary Research in Music Media and Technology (CIRMMT) · Sounds in the City · AIRS thematic network

Research Overview

My research begins with the challenge of urban sound: how we can move beyond controlling unwanted noise toward creating better-sounding, healthier cities. Addressing this challenge requires more than technical knowledge about sound. It raises broader questions about how people understand complex issues, how specialized knowledge is represented and shared, how alternatives can be explored and compared, and how new approaches can become meaningful within professional and institutional contexts.

My research has grown from these questions. Through the design and study of interactive and immersive systems, I investigate how complex and specialized knowledge can be made more experiential, understandable, and usable. Beginning from soundscape, this work connects human–computer interaction and Information Studies with questions of knowledge translation, capacity building, and professional practice, while increasingly engaging with broader socio-technical questions about how people, technologies, expertise, and organizations interact.

City Ditty is the primary research platform through which many of these questions have been developed and studied. The interactive soundscape simulator allows people to experience, explore, design, and evaluate possible urban soundscapes, while providing a shared platform for research into interaction, learning, collaboration, and professional practice.

The research themes below emerged through this problem-oriented programme and describe several of the broader questions that now extend beyond any single system or study.

Core Research Themes

My current research can be organized around four closely connected themes: Human–Soundscape Interaction, Interactive & Immersive Systems, Enabling Participation, and Research into Practice. Together, they trace a progression from soundscape interaction and system design to broader questions of participation and professional practice.

1

Human–Soundscape Interaction

Urban soundscape is the primary problem domain from which much of my research has developed. I explore how interactive systems can help people experience, investigate, and shape soundscape environments.

  • Sound-based interaction: Investigate how interactive systems can support active engagement with sound and listening within environmental context.
  • Beyond simulation: Move from representing possible soundscapes to interacting with, changing, and comparing them.
  • People, technology, and environment: Examine how interaction shapes the way soundscapes are experienced, understood, and explored.

Related research

  • Initial City Ditty evaluation — demonstrates how non-specialists can learn soundscape concepts, manipulate a simulated environment, and create their own soundscape designs through interaction.
  • Sound-based interaction chapter — places this work within a broader HCI perspective on interaction through sound, spanning soundscapes, interactive sound art, and digital musical instruments.
2

Interactive & Immersive Systems

Working through these soundscape problems has raised broader questions about interactive and immersive system design. I examine how technologies and representations can be matched to what people need to understand or do.

  • Problem-led design: Choose and develop technology in response to the problem and activity it needs to support.
  • Representation for interaction: Design information so people can explore, manipulate, compare, and act on it.
  • Modality for the activity: Desktop, immersive VR, and other interfaces offer different strengths; greater immersion is not inherently better.

Related research

  • Desktop–HMD comparison — compares interaction, presence, spatial understanding, engagement, usability, and listening experience across desktop and immersive VR.
  • City Ditty in immersive VR — introduces the HMD version of City Ditty through a VRST demo and poster, presenting the platform and its broader uses in soundscape design, knowledge sharing, and professional contexts.
  • Soundscape simulator design considerations — examines how much and what kinds of realism are appropriate in soundscape simulation through the lenses of plausibility, hyperrealism, and ecological validity.
3

Enabling Participation

I explore how interactive and experiential approaches can widen meaningful participation in complex issues by improving access to specialized knowledge and building people's capacity to engage with it.

  • Knowledge translation: Represent specialized knowledge in forms that are easier to understand, experience, and communicate across different areas of expertise.
  • Capacity building: Support learning, awareness, and confidence so people are better equipped to explore alternatives and engage with complex issues.
  • Shared understanding: Create common points of reference that help people with different expertise communicate, compare possibilities, and participate in discussion and decision-making.

Related research

  • Early professional needs assessment — uses a knowledge-mobilization workshop with professionals of the built environment to identify needs and requirements that informed the first version of City Ditty.
  • Interdisciplinary urban park design — demonstrates a site-specific workflow in which collaborators adapt and use City Ditty to support soundscape design within a new urban context.
  • Comparative soundscape experiment — demonstrates how environments created in City Ditty can be reused as controlled study conditions, lowering technical barriers for researchers conducting interactive soundscape studies.
4

Research into Practice

I examine how interactive systems and research knowledge can connect meaningfully with professional activity, collaboration, and decision-making.

  • Earlier consideration: Support exploration of complex issues while alternatives are still open to discussion and change.
  • Complementing expertise: Help non-specialists engage more effectively with specialized issues without replacing professional judgment.
  • Institutional support: Consider how roles, resources, organizational support, technical infrastructure, and existing ways of working shape what is possible.

Related research

  • City-maker stakeholder study — examines how interactive soundscape simulation can support capacity building among professionals through education, communication, workflow integration, and professional practice.
  • Emerging technologies and soundscape practice — examines professional soundscape practice through an emerging-technologies framework, considering how interactive simulation could challenge established approaches and fit within the wider systemic environment.

Emerging Directions

Alongside these core themes, several newer directions extend the research into additional applications, contexts, and questions. Some build directly from City Ditty, while others reflect broader methodological, educational, and theoretical questions arising from the work.

Interactive Soundscape Evaluation

City Ditty is also being developed as a platform for researchers to create and run comparative soundscape studies.

  • From design to evaluation: Reuse and modify designed soundscape environments as controlled study conditions.
  • Lower technical barriers: Enable researchers to configure comparative studies without building a new interactive system for each experiment.
  • Replication and extension: Support studies that can be repeated, adapted, and extended across new participants, settings, and research questions.

Related research

  • Comparative soundscape experiment — demonstrates how environments created in City Ditty can be reused as controlled study conditions for comparative evaluation.

Localization & Cross-Cultural Research

City Ditty now supports localization for different geographic and cultural contexts, allowing collaborators to develop place-specific environments and content that can be integrated into the broader platform.

  • Localization: Support collaborators in adapting environments, sound sources, and activities to reflect local people and places.
  • Context matters: Recognize that soundscapes are shaped by local activities, meanings, and environmental conditions.
  • Cross-cultural potential: Multiple localized versions may support future comparisons across populations and cultural settings.

Related research

  • Locale adaptation and simulation setup — documents practical steps to adapt City Ditty to a new urban and soundscape context using local spatial, audio, and visual assets.
  • Participatory design competition — documents the interdisciplinary workshop and student competition that followed the localized simulation setup.

Interdisciplinary Research Practice

I am exploring how interdisciplinary design research affects students and early-career researchers, particularly when projects require them to combine technical development, design, and deep domain knowledge.

  • Researcher experience: Examine the demands created by unusually broad skill sets, responsibilities, and interdisciplinary roles.
  • Training and transition: Consider how graduate education prepares researchers for this work and for the career transitions that follow.
  • Academic structures: Explore how funding, supervision, continuity, and institutional pathways can better support both researchers and the work they produce.

Related research

  • Extensible by Design, Unsustainable by Structure — examines tensions faced by graduate researchers developing extensible interdisciplinary artefacts, including responsibility, continuity, funding, disciplinary fit, and career transitions.

Socio-Technical Design

I am developing a broader socio-technical design framework that draws together lessons from across my research programme.

  • Beyond the artefact: Consider interactive systems as part of wider environments shaped by people, practices, organizations, and resources.
  • Design for context: Account for the institutional and practical conditions that influence how systems are used and sustained.
  • From prototype to practice: Examine what is needed for research systems to contribute meaningfully beyond a successful study or demonstration.
